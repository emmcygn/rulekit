rulekit

Eligibility criteria are the most consequential logic in a clinical trial. Almost everywhere, they are prose in a Word document.

This is a thesis about treating them as code instead — versioned, tested, conflict-checked — told in one scroll. Agents propose evidence. Humans confirm it. A deterministic engine is the only thing that ever decides.

scroll — about ten minutes, best read in order

01the problem

Law, written as prose, copied by hand

Eligibility criteriawho may join a trial decide who gets an experimental drug. They are not versioned as behaviour, not tested, not checked for contradiction — and when they change, nobody can say who is affected without a manual chart review.

The protocolthe trial's rulebook document states each criterion once, in a paragraph. Then every team that needs it makes its own working copy — and watch what happens to one number on the way down:

Drift like this surfaces as a protocol amendmenta formal protocol change — re-consenting, re-training, re-printing, IRB re-review. The Tufts Center for the Study of Drug Development put numbers on it:

A recurring cause is eligibility logic: a threshold that contradicts another threshold, a criterion nobody realised excluded a third of the screening pool, a change whose effect on already-enrolled participants surfaces only after it ships. Two of those three are catchable before the protocol leaves the building — by a tool that can read the criteria as logic. So the first move is to make them logic.

02prose → code

One sentence becomes one rule

Keep scrolling and watch a real criterion cross over. The trick is not the YAML — it is that the protocol's exact words make the trip too.

“eGFR at least 30 mL/min/1.73m²”

Inclusion criterion I3, as the protocol writes it. eGFR is a kidney-function lab value. Now scroll: each field of the rule lights up as it appears.

idA stable name. It appears in every trace, coverage report and diff downstream, so renaming one is a breaking change — ids are treated as API.
ref · kindref is the protocol's own numbering (I3), not ours. kind says which way the criterion points — inclusion admits, exclusion removes. That orientation matters enormously in chapter 03.
verbatim — the load-bearing fieldThe protocol's exact sentence, never edited to fit the language. The words travel with the logic, so the file stays auditable against the source document forever. If a criterion can't be expressed at all, it is kept as unmodeled: true with its verbatim text — carried, never dropped.
when — the executable meaning“at least 30” becomes op: gte, value: 30 against the declared fact egfr. Sentence and semantics now live in one diffable, reviewable file. A human wrote this line and a human reviews it — no model translates protocols by itself.

03the engine

A condition language you can hold in your head

Rules compose through exactly three combinators — all, any, not — over five families of leaf. Nothing else. No arbitrary expressions, ever.

The evaluator is a pure function of (rule set, one patient's facts): same inputs, same verdict, forever. Nothing calls a clock, nothing calls a random number, nothing calls a model. Now the interesting part — what happens when a fact is missing. Which, for real patients, is constantly.

03three values, not two

patient SYN-042 · confirmed facts age: 63  ·  lvef: 38  ·  egfr: — absent
step 1 · a real patientSYN-042 is one of the synthetic fixtures. Their chart has age and LVEF, but no eGFR result. In the format's own words: an absent key is unknown, never false — the single most important sentence in the spec. Scroll on.
step 2 · facts flow into leavesEach leaf compares one fact. age = 63 ≥ 18 → true. lvef = 38 ≤ 40 → true. And egfr? There is nothing to compare. The leaf evaluates unknown — a first-class third value, not an error and not a default.
step 3 · unknown propagates upall(true, true, unknown) = unknown. This is strong Kleene logicthree-valued truth tables: unknown flows through the gates with no imputation and no silent coercion. The criterion's honest answer is “we can't tell yet”, so that is the answer.
all(true, unknown) = unknown
any(false, unknown) = unknown
all(false, unknown) = false
any(true, unknown) = true
step 4 · the four gates worth memorisingThe top two say uncertainty survives. The bottom two are the useful absorbers: all(false, unknown) = false means one confirmed failure settles it — no chart review needed — and any(true, unknown) = true likewise.
a two-valued engineegfr absent → treated as false → the exclusion egfr lt 45 never fires → the patient silently passes a renal-safety check that was never actually checked.
rulekitegfr absent → unknown → the criterion is unknown → the patient lands in undetermined, the pile that says “a human must look”.
step 5 · the one everyone gets wrongMissing data never silently passes. Boolean logic has no way to say “we don't know”, so two-valued screeners quietly convert ignorance into an answer. The third value exists precisely so they can't.

03orientation & the verdict

Fail beats unknown beats pass

One twist before the verdict: a criterion's result is not its condition's value, because exclusions invert. When an exclusion's condition is true, the exclusion has fired — and the patient fails it.

Then the whole patient is aggregated by one rule, first match wins: any fail → ineligible; else any unknown → undetermined; else eligible. Watch it cascade:

04conflict detection

Proving contradictions without inventing them

Because the language is closed, rules check can do interval arithmetic on the criteria themselves. What follows is ordinary inclusion/exclusion overlap — and why treating it as a contradiction was a dangerous false positive.

egfr-min I3 · inclusion

“eGFR at least 30 mL/min/1.73m²”

when: { fact: egfr, op: gte, value: 30 }

renal-safety E3 · exclusion

“eGFR below 45 at screening”

when: { fact: egfr, op: lt, value: 45 }

two authors, eleven pages apartBoth criteria are individually sensible: admit adequate kidney function, exclude fragile kidney function. They were written by different people in different sections of the protocol. Scroll — the checker reads them as geometry.
the inclusion, as an intervalgte 30 becomes the interval [30, ∞) — a closed left bracket at 30, unbounded above. Only patients inside it can pass I3.
the exclusion, as an intervallt 45 becomes (−∞, 45) — everything strictly below 45. Any patient inside it gets thrown out by E3. Now look at where the bars overlap.
0 conflict(s), 0 warning(s) — static analysis covers interval and direct code-set proofs; incomplete criteria are labeled above.
the corrected findingPassing an inclusion and then firing an exclusion is exactly how eligibility logic works. The overlap identifies patients E3 removes; it does not prove the rule set admits nobody. CI blocks only when inclusions are mutually impossible or an exclusion covers the entire admitted domain.

frail-long-stay exclusion · two facts

“Age over 80 AND index hospitalisation longer than 21 days”

when: { all: [ age gt 80, index_hospital_days gt 21 ] }

no band claim

The checker emits analysis-incomplete, not a false proof.

the honesty beat“For all inputs” is a sentence that must never be wrong. Necessary constraints outside opaque any/not branches are still checked, but unsupported logic receives an explicit scope finding. The checker uses interval and direct code-set proofs — explicitly not an SMT solver.

05the patients

The engine meets people

Rules and conflicts are abstractions until a patient walks through them. Here are three cases from the ten hand-written fixture patients the workbench ships with — every one of them synthetic, and every number below the engine's actual output.

Each case runs the same way: the patient's facts flow through the criterion rows, one mark lands per criterion — solid for pass, outline for fail, hatch for unknown — and the tracethe engine's printed reasoning types out the arithmetic. The trace is the point. A verdict you cannot explain is a verdict you cannot audit.

The condition language runs on three-valued logicpass, fail, unknown — Kleene's. A question the data cannot answer evaluates unknown — not no, not probably no. The engine refuses to guess.

E1 stays hatched in her trace even though E3 already decided her verdict — unknowns are never coerced to fit the outcome. When nothing else decides, unknown reaches the top: three of the ten fixtures end undetermined, a first-class outcome, not an error.

the one-status-per-patient law A patient's band comes from the engine's verdict and nothing else — the CLI and the workbench can never tell different stories.
That sentence is a scar, not a slogan. A review of this repo caught the funnel draining patients in criterion order while the CLI bucketed by overall verdict — on the 100-patient commander-hf corpus the two disagreed on every headline number (0 vs 2 potentially eligible, 97 vs 92 screen failures), and swapping two criteria in the YAML — a no-op to the engine — moved a patient between the demo's two biggest buckets. The fix is one rule in src/core/attrition.ts: the waterfall may only distribute patients who are already screen failures.

07the amendment

The day the protocol changes

A protocol amendmentformal change to trial rules is where prose criteria hurt most. A substantial one carries a median direct cost of $0K in Phase II and $0K in Phase III, and roughly 0% are judged avoidable (Tufts CSDD).

DEMO-HF-001 moves from v2.0 to v3.0 (Amendment 2): one new exclusion drops in, one washout window widens. In a Word document, the impact question — who changes answer? — takes a manual chart review. In a rule file, it is a diff. Not a diff of the text: a behavioural diffwhich patients change answer.

08the other side

Where facts come from

Rules were the easy half. Real eligibility data is locked in narrative notes, and the tempting move is to let a language model read the chart and answer “is this patient eligible?” rulekit refuses that shape.

Instead there is a facts compilernotes in, typed facts out: the model reads a note and proposes a typed fact — a name, a value, a confidence score, and a verbatim quote from the source document. Watch it work on a synthetic echo report for a patient you have already met.

A fact that can't cite its source doesn't exist. The gate is mechanical: the quote must appear verbatim in the document, or the proposal never reaches a human.

The rejected proposal above is illustrative. On the recorded eval set — 10 authored notes, 61 proposals — 58 survived the gate (95.1%); it caught 2 wrong facts and over-blocked 1 right one.
